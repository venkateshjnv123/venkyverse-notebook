Our service was choking. We thought circuit breakers would save us.

Turns out, that's only half the story. 😅

We called a third-party Service B from our main flow. Service B was unreliable — down often, and when it wasn't down, responses were taking **30+ seconds**.

![The problem: Service B taking 30+ seconds, choking Service A under load](/images/circuit-breaker-problem.png)

We added a strict **500ms timeout** first — if Service B didn't respond in time, we cancelled the request. Failures dropped, but not enough.

## The pattern people know

A circuit breaker watches for failures. After N consecutive failures, it opens and returns errors immediately without attempting the call. After a cooldown, it lets a small batch through to test recovery.

Clean. Elegant. Widely taught.

Here's what's missing: **the circuit breaker can only open after failures accumulate**. If calls are timing out slowly — hanging for 30 seconds each — you accumulate 60 seconds of blocked threads before the circuit trips. By then, your connection pool is exhausted.

![Circuit breaker states: Closed → Open → Half-Open, with 25% traffic ramp-up](/images/circuit-breaker-states.png)

## The three states

**CLOSED** — everything normal, requests flow through to Service B.

**OPEN** — too many failures detected, we stop all requests to Service B and return errors immediately.

**HALF-OPEN** — after a cooldown, we test with a small batch. Instead of going all-in, we gradually ramped traffic:

```
25% → 50% → 75% → 100%
```

If failures increase → back to OPEN. If Service B holds stable → fully CLOSED.

<Callout type="warning" label="The lesson the hard way">
A slow dependency is more dangerous than a fast-failing one. Fast failures trip your circuit breaker. Slow responses drain your resources while everything looks green on your metrics.
</Callout>

## The resilience stack

After this incident, resilience became three layers working together — not alternatives:

**Layer 1: Timeouts** — every external call gets an aggressive timeout. Not the framework default. A number you calculated. If it hasn't returned in 500ms, kill it. Fast failure > slow success.

**Layer 2: Circuit breakers** — now that you have timeouts, failures accumulate quickly. Configure breakers to count timeouts too, not just exceptions.

**Layer 3: Traffic ramp-up** — when a circuit closes, don't immediately send full traffic. A service that just recovered is fragile. A sudden burst often kills it again.

```
Request → [Timeout: 500ms] → [Circuit Breaker] → Service B
                                    ↓
                         [Ramp-up on recovery: 25→50→75→100%]
```

<Aside>
Most teams implement layer 2 and skip layers 1 and 3. Layer 1 is the most impactful per line of code.
</Aside>

## Results

After wiring all three together:

- Failure logs reduced by **40%** ✅
- Service stayed healthy under load ✅
- Next bad-dependency day was boring — pricing calls failed fast, circuit tripped, fallback showed, nothing else was affected

<Callout type="success" label="The pattern that works">
Timeouts make failures fast. Circuit breakers prevent calling a broken service. Traffic ramp-up protects recovering services. All three, in sequence.
</Callout>
